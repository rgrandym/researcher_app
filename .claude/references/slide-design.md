# Slide design

How `/slides` and the `slide-reviewer` judge a deck. A slide is read in seconds, from
a distance or a shared screen, while someone talks over it. Every rule below follows
from that.

## 1. One message per slide

- **The title states the point.** "Revenue triples by 2028 on two contracts" carries
  the slide; "Revenue" labels it. A reader who sees only the titles should get the
  argument. Rewriting a title changes words, so it is a content change the author
  accepts like any other, and its claim must already be in the deck or its sources.
- **Everything else on the slide supports that point.** A box that argues something
  else belongs on another slide, in the notes, or nowhere.
- **Density:** about 40 words of body text is comfortable; 60 is the ceiling the
  inspection flags. Split a crowded slide before shrinking its text.

## 2. Hierarchy

- The eye should land on the title, then the one figure or visual, then the
  supporting text. Size, weight, colour and position create that order; use as few of
  them as achieve it.
- **Type scale:** four to six sizes for the whole deck, in steady steps (for a 16:9
  deck at 960 x 540 pt, for example 40 / 28 / 20 / 16 / 12). Near-duplicates (20 and
  20.16, 28 and 28.45) come from pasting and read as mistakes; snap them to the scale.
- **Minimum sizes:** body text 16 pt or more; 14 pt for dense supporting text; 10 to
  12 pt only for labels, sources and footers. A pitch deck shared as a PDF tolerates
  smaller text than one projected, but never below 9 pt.
- **Typefaces:** one family, two at most (one for headings, one for text). Weight and
  size do the work a second or third family would.

## 3. Grid and alignment

- Every slide shares the same margins and columns. Titles start at the same point on
  every slide of the same kind; body text shares a left edge.
- Edges that nearly line up (a few points apart) look careless; snap them together.
  Edges that are meant to differ should differ clearly (a column, an indent).
- Leave room. A margin of about 5 to 6 percent of the slide width on each side, and
  space between groups larger than space within them.

## 4. Colour

- A deck runs on its brand palette: one or two dark neutrals for text, one or two
  light neutrals for backgrounds, and one or two accents. An accent marks the one
  thing to notice; used everywhere, it marks nothing.
- **Contrast:** text against its background at least 4.5:1 (3:1 for text 24 pt and
  above). Mid-grey text on a light grey panel is the commonest failure.
- Near-duplicate colours (two greys a few steps apart, used for the same job) come
  from pasting; merge them with a deck-wide replacement.
- Colour never carries meaning alone: a label or position says it too.

## 5. Figures and tables on slides

- One message per chart, written as its title. Highlight the series that carries it
  in the accent and grey the rest.
- Label directly on the chart; a legend makes the reader match colours.
- A table on a slide shows a few rows the talk is about. A full table belongs in an
  appendix or a handout.
- Numbers keep their sources. A redesign never changes a figure, a unit or a
  citation; those changes go through the evidence pipeline.

## 6. Consistency across the deck

- Slides of the same kind (section opener, content, team, figure, closing) share a
  layout. A deck needs few layouts, applied the same way every time.
- Logos, footers and slide numbers sit in the same place on every slide that has them.

## 7. Pitch decks

- The usual order: problem, solution, why now, market, product, business model,
  traction, competition, team, financials, the ask. Reorder only with the author's
  agreement; the order is an argument.
- The ask and the headline numbers are the slides an investor remembers: they get the
  clearest hierarchy and the most room.

## 8. What a redesign may change, and what it may not

| May change, once accepted | Never changes without going through the evidence pipeline |
|---|---|
| Position and size of boxes, pictures and charts | Any figure, unit, date, name or claim |
| Fonts, sizes, weight, colours, fills, alignment | Sources and citations |
| Theme colours and fonts; deck-wide colour and font replacements | The meaning of a title or a sentence |
| Slide order (author decides) | Removing a slide's content |

A redesign keeps the author's brand unless the author asks for a new one. When the
deck has no consistent brand, propose one built from the colours and fonts it already
uses most.
